# Headline and tagline copy (C-n)

## Techniques
- C-1 Brevity: 3 to 6 words, so the line fits working memory. Source: https://greenlitcontent.com/marketing/what-makes-a-slogan-successful-the-ultimate-guide
- C-2 Rhythm and parallelism: repeat one sentence pattern so the idea scans like verse and sticks. Sources: https://lancaster.ac.uk/fass/projects/stylistics/topic3a/persil.htm and https://admiredleadership.substack.com/p/parallelism-and-making-ideas-memorable
- C-3 Rhyme and alliteration: patterned sound is processed more fluently; one cited study reports about 38% better recall. Source: greenlitcontent URL under C-1 (secondary summary; treat the figure as indicative).
- C-4 Antithesis and reversal: set two opposites side by side (quiet / loud) or flip the expected phrase. Source: https://writingcenter.tamu.edu/about-us/in-a-word/rhetorical-devices
- C-5 Name the customer's tension: say the feeling they already have, then refuse it. Practice, uncited.
- C-6 Question then answer: pose their worry, reply in two words ("Hidden gem? Not anymore."). Practice, uncited.
- C-7 Verb plus benefit: an imperative that carries the outcome (e.g. Mailchimp "Send better email."). Practice, uncited.

## Page heading system
- The hero h1 is the one broad catchphrase; it states the theme, not the process.
- Section heads must not reuse each other's key words (check every h1/h2/h3 together before writing a new one).
- Each head has one job: promise, proof, process or reassurance. Never two.
- A lede adds a fact (who, how, what you get), never restates its head.
- Two-line heroes: line 1 sets up, line 2 is the accented payoff.

## 6-point headline check
1. Six words or fewer?
2. Does it use at least one of C-2 to C-4 audibly (read it aloud)?
3. Does it name a tension or outcome the visitor would say themselves?
4. Is it concrete, with an image or action, not an abstract noun like "growth"?
5. Does it share no key word with any other heading on the page?
6. Is every claim provable, with no "best" or "#1"?
